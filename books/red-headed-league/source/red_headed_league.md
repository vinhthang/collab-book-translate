# Chapter I

I had called upon my friend, Mr. Sherlock Holmes, one day in the autumn of last year and found him in deep conversation with a very stout, florid-faced, elderly gentleman with fiery red hair. With an apology for my intrusion, I was about to withdraw when Holmes pulled me abruptly into the room and closed the door behind me. "You could not possibly have come at a better time, my dear Watson," he said cordially. The stout gentleman half rose from his chair and gave a bob of greeting, with a quick little questioning glance from his small fat-encircled eyes. "You may address me as Mr. Jabez Wilson," he said. "I keep a pawnbroker's shop in Saxe-Coburg Square, and I have come to you because of a most singular business."

"I have a young assistant, Vincent Spaulding, who is very clever in his way," Wilson continued, "and it was he who first called my attention to an advertisement in the paper. 'To the Red-Headed League: On account of the bequest of the late Ezekiah Hopkins, of Lebanon, Pennsylvania, U.S.A., there is now another vacancy open which entitles a member of the League to a salary of four pounds a week for purely nominal services.' My hair is of the reddest possible colour, and Spaulding told me that there could not be a better candidate, so I applied and was accepted by the manager, Mr. Duncan Ross, after a short interview in which he examined the colour of my hair with the greatest care."

"The work was to copy out the Encyclopaedia Britannica, four hours a day, in an office in Pope's Court, Fleet Street. I was to be in the office, or at least in the building, the whole time. If I left, I forfeited my whole position forever. The will was very clear upon that point. I worked there for eight weeks, copying out Abbots, Archery, and Armour, and I was paid my four pounds every Saturday. Then, last Saturday, I found a notice on the office door: 'The Red-Headed League is dissolved. October 9, 1890.' The office was empty, the manager had vanished, and no one in the street could tell me where he had gone. That is why I have come to you, Mr. Holmes, for I cannot understand what it all means."

# Chapter II

Holmes listened to the whole story without moving a muscle of his face, but his eyes glittered as he asked his questions. He wanted to know everything about Vincent Spaulding: his age, his habits, his wages, and what he did in the shop when Wilson was away. Wilson told him that Spaulding took only half wages, that he was very fond of photography, and that he spent much of his time in the cellar, developing his photographs in the dark. Holmes smiled at this, and at the mention of the cellar he tapped his fingers together in a way I knew well. He asked also about the shops on either side of the pawnbroker's, and learned that on one side was a tobacconist and on the other a branch of the City and Suburban Bank.

That afternoon Holmes took me to Saxe-Coburg Square, where he examined the pavement before the pawnbroker's shop with the greatest attention, knocking upon it with his stick and listening to the sound it made. He then walked to the bank, and under the pretext of business he asked the manager to show him the cellar, which he examined as carefully as a man examining a room in which he expected to spend the night. On our way back he explained nothing, but he told me to meet him at Baker Street that evening at ten o'clock, and to bring my revolver. "There is a bank robbery to be prevented tonight," he said, "and the robber is none other than John Clay, the most dangerous criminal in London, who has been working under the name of Vincent Spaulding."

"The League was a trick, Watson, and a clever one," Holmes said as we waited in the dark. "Clay needed Wilson out of his shop for four hours every day, so that he could dig a tunnel from the cellar to the vault of the City and Suburban Bank. The salary was bait, and the Encyclopaedia was the excuse. Spaulding's knees, which I noticed when he showed me his trousers this morning, were worn and stained with the clay of the tunnel. The League dissolved the moment the tunnel was finished, because Clay needed Wilson no longer. Tonight the French gold will be moved into the vault, and Clay will come through his tunnel to take it. We shall be waiting for him."

# Chapter III

We sat in the darkness of the bank vault with Mr. Merryweather, the director, and Inspector Jones of Scotland Yard, our revolvers ready and our breathing held. The night wore on in silence, and at last, from the floor beneath us, came a faint scraping sound, and then a square of light appeared as a flagstone was lifted. A clean-shaven, youthful face peered out, and then a man sprang up into the vault, with a second man close behind him. "It is John Clay!" cried Holmes, springing forward with his revolver levelled. Clay turned like a rat in a trap, and for a moment there was a struggle, but Jones seized him by the collar, and the second man was taken as well. The tunnel behind them ran straight back to the cellar of the pawnbroker's shop in Saxe-Coburg Square.

When the prisoners had been taken away, Holmes explained the whole affair to the astonished bank director. "The Red-Headed League was invented to keep Mr. Wilson away from his shop," he said. "Clay, under the name of Vincent Spaulding, took half wages so that Wilson would keep him, and spent his days digging the tunnel while Wilson copied the Encyclopaedia. The League was dissolved the day the tunnel was finished, because the work was done and Wilson was no longer needed. If we had waited another night, the French gold would have been gone, and Clay would have been a rich man."

As we walked home through the quiet streets, I asked Holmes how he had known. "I had observed that Spaulding's trousers were worn at the knees, that he was fond of photography, which gave him an excuse to be in the cellar, and that the bank stood next door to the pawnshop," he said. "The rest was a chain of reasoning, and the chain held. It is a pity, Watson, that Clay is a criminal, for he has the brains of a statesman. Well, perhaps, after all, his work is of some little use, for he has shown us how a man may be ruined by his own hair." And with that, we turned into Baker Street, and the case of the Red-Headed League was at an end.
