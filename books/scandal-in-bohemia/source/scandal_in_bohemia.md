# Chapter I

To Sherlock Holmes she is always the woman. I have seldom heard him mention her under any other name. In his eyes she eclipses and predominates the whole of her sex. It was not that he felt any emotion akin to love for Irene Adler. All emotions, and that one particularly, were abhorrent to his cold, precise but admirably balanced mind.

One night—it was on the twentieth of March, 1888—I was returning from a journey to a patient (for I had now returned to civil practice), when my way led me through Baker Street. As I passed the well-remembered door, which must always be associated in my mind with my wooing, and with the dark incidents of the Study in Scarlet, I was seized with a keen desire to see Holmes again, and to know how he was employing his extraordinary powers.

A slow and heavy step, which had been heard upon the stairs and in the passage, paused immediately outside the door. Then there was a loud and authoritative tap. "Come in!" said Holmes. A man entered who could hardly have been less than six feet six inches in height, with the chest and limbs of a Hercules. Looking from one to the other of us, he said: "You may address me as the Count Von Kramm, a Bohemian nobleman, though I speak to you on behalf of His Majesty the King of Bohemia."

# Chapter II

"Five years ago," continued our visitor, "during a lengthy visit to Warsaw, I made the acquaintance of the well-known adventuress, Irene Adler. The name is no doubt familiar to you." "Kindly look her up in my index, Doctor," murmured Holmes without opening his eyes. For many years he had adopted a system of docketing all paragraphs concerning men and things, so that it was difficult to name a subject or a person on which he could not at once furnish information.

The next afternoon at five o'clock Sherlock Holmes returned to Baker Street dressed as a drunken-looking groom, ill-kempt and side-whiskered, with an inflamed face and disreputable clothes. "You see, Watson," he chuckled as he threw himself into an armchair, "I spent the whole morning around Briony Lodge in Serpentine Avenue. Not only did I learn every habit of the household, but by a stroke of luck I served as the compulsory witness at the sudden marriage of Miss Irene Adler to Mr. Godfrey Norton at the Church of St. Monica."

"Tonight at seven," said Holmes briskly, "you will accompany me to Briony Lodge. I shall wear the simple black frock-coat of an amiable Nonconformist clergyman. When I raise my hand and cry 'Fire!', you will throw this ordinary plumber's smoke-rocket through the open window and immediately walk to the corner of the street, where I shall rejoin you in ten minutes."

# Chapter III

The alarm of fire was admirably done. No sooner had the cry been raised and the thick cloud of smoke billowed through the sitting-room window of Briony Lodge than we saw Irene Adler dart automatically toward a recessed sliding panel beside the mantelpiece to rescue what she valued most. Satisfied that the hiding-place of the photograph was betrayed, Holmes slipped out of the house, shedding his clerical disguise as we walked back toward Baker Street.

Yet as we turned into our own doorway at 221B Baker Street, a slim youth in an ulster coat hurried past us along the pavement and called out in a clear, musical voice: "Good-night, Mister Sherlock Holmes." Holmes started and stared back into the lamp-lit fog. "I've heard that voice before," he muttered thoughtfully. "Now, I wonder who the deuce that could have been?"

When His Majesty the King of Bohemia accompanied us to Briony Lodge the following morning, the bird had flown. In the secret recess behind the sliding panel lay not the cabinet photograph of the two together, but a portrait of Irene Adler alone in evening dress, accompanied by a letter addressed to Sherlock Holmes in which she revealed that she had penetrated his disguise and had departed England forever with her husband, promising never to use the photograph against the King. And that was how a great scandal threatened to affect the kingdom of Bohemia, and how the best plans of Mr. Sherlock Holmes were beaten by a woman's wit.
